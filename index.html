About Me

From field reality to business decisions.

I work at the intersection of business research, data, and practical problem-solving.

My experience has taken me from working directly with microenterprises and communities to supporting structured research, financial-literacy programs, product validation, and business-growth initiatives. Across these engagements, I have learned that meaningful analysis rarely starts with a spreadsheet - it starts by understanding the people, processes, constraints, and decisions behind the numbers.

I enjoy working where a business problem is not yet clearly defined: understanding what is happening on the ground, structuring the information, finding patterns, and turning those findings into something a client or stakeholder can actually act on.

What I have learned

Good analysis starts with how you collect the data. Who you ask, where you ask them, and how you phrase the question shape the answer. Small group discussions, simple questionnaires, and trying a product in a real home all showed me things a desk study would have missed.

Context decides the method. Sensitive topics need a comfortable setting. Beginners need plain language. A product needs real testing, not just opinions. I learned to choose the approach that fits the people and the problem before choosing the tool.

Findings only matter if someone can act on them. A report is not the end product. The decision it helps someone make is. I learned to turn raw responses into a clear pattern, and then into a recommendation with a next step, including recommending a careful phased start when a full launch isn't yet justified.

Different audiences need different framing. The same evidence has to be presented one way to a funder, another way to a government body, and another to a business owner. Knowing what each of them needs to decide is part of the analysis.

Documentation is part of the work. Clean files, version control, and consistent reporting keep a project reliable when many people, sessions, and data sources are involved. This matters most as the work grows in scale.

Learning from the ground and from my managers. Working under experienced supervisors showed me how to take feedback, ask better questions, and move from supporting a project to owning more of the deliverables.

These experiences have shaped how I approach analysis:

The value of analysis is not in finding an answer.
It is in helping someone make a better decision
